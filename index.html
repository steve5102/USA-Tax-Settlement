How Much Does the IRS Fresh Start Program Cost?

Understand the possible IRS fees, ongoing interest and penalties, and the separate cost of getting professional help with a tax debt resolution.

◷ 8-minute read ▤ Practical cost guide Updated for 2026 IRS fee information

If you have searched for the cost of the IRS Fresh Start Program, you may be expecting one fixed price. There isn't one universal Fresh Start enrollment fee. The phrase is commonly used to describe several IRS tax-relief options, and the amount you may pay depends on which option fits your circumstances.

Your total cost may include the tax balance itself, interest and penalties that continue to accrue, an IRS payment-plan setup fee, an Offer in Compromise application payment, and fees charged by a tax professional if you choose to hire one.

The short answer: Some IRS payment options have no setup fee, while qualifying long-term installment agreements have a setup fee. An Offer in Compromise generally has a $205 application fee and an initial offer payment, unless you qualify for the low-income exception. Professional representation is a separate cost.

What does “Fresh Start” actually mean?

Fresh Start is not a single program with one application price or one guaranteed outcome. The term is used for IRS policies and resolution routes that can help eligible taxpayers address unpaid federal tax. Depending on the case, a taxpayer may be able to pay over time, propose a settlement for less than the full balance, request penalty relief, or ask the IRS to temporarily delay collection.

That distinction matters because each route has different eligibility rules, payment obligations, and potential costs. The right starting point is to understand the options rather than assume that every taxpayer qualifies for the same arrangement.

1. Installment agreements: paying the balance over time

An installment agreement allows an eligible taxpayer to pay an IRS balance in scheduled payments instead of paying it all at once. The setup fee depends on the plan type, how you apply, and the payment method. The IRS may also continue charging interest and applicable penalties until the balance is paid.

2. Offer in Compromise: applying to settle for less

An Offer in Compromise (OIC) is an agreement that may allow an eligible taxpayer to settle a tax liability for less than the full amount owed. It is not automatic debt forgiveness. The IRS evaluates the taxpayer's ability to pay, income, expenses, asset equity, and other relevant circumstances.

For most applicants, the IRS lists a $205 application fee. Applicants generally must also include an initial payment based on the offer and payment method selected:

  • Lump-sum cash offer: generally requires 20% of the offer amount with the application; the remaining amount is paid in five or fewer payments if accepted.
  • Periodic payment offer: generally requires the first proposed payment with the application and continued payments while the IRS reviews the offer.
  • Low-income exception: qualifying applicants may not have to pay the application fee or initial offer payment. Review the IRS instructions to determine whether you qualify.

The application fee and offer payments are not the same as a tax professional's fee. If you hire a representative, ask for a written explanation of what their service includes and whether additional work will cost extra.

3. Interest and penalties can increase the total

A low setup fee does not mean resolving tax debt will cost only a few dollars. Unless a specific rule or relief decision applies, interest and applicable penalties may continue to accrue while a balance remains unpaid. The longer the debt remains outstanding, the more important it becomes to understand how those additions affect the total.

The exact amount depends on the tax type, dates, payments, and applicable IRS rates. Rates can change, so avoid relying on a general percentage from an old article or estimate. Review your IRS account and current IRS interest and penalty guidance for figures that apply to your situation.

4. What does professional tax relief help cost?

Hiring a tax attorney, enrolled agent, CPA, or other qualified tax professional is optional in many situations. Some taxpayers can review their account and apply for an IRS payment plan themselves. Others prefer assistance because their case involves multiple tax years, unfiled returns, collection notices, a levy or lien issue, an audit dispute, or a possible Offer in Compromise.

There is no government-set price for private representation. Fees vary with the provider, the complexity of the case, the amount of work required, and the resolution strategy. A simple review is different from preparing financial disclosures, negotiating with the IRS, responding to collection action, or handling an appeal.

Questions to ask before hiring a firm

  • What work is included in the quoted fee?
  • Is the fee fixed, hourly, or divided into stages?
  • Are tax return preparation and back-tax filing included?
  • Will additional IRS notices or negotiations create extra charges?
  • Who will handle the case, and what credentials do they hold?
  • What happens if the proposed resolution is not accepted?

Be cautious of anyone promising a specific reduction before reviewing your financial information. No private firm can guarantee that the IRS will accept a particular settlement.

5. Eligibility and preparation affect the route you can use

Not every taxpayer qualifies for every option. The IRS may require required returns to be filed and current estimated tax or withholding obligations to be addressed before considering certain arrangements. An Offer in Compromise has its own financial review and application requirements.

Before choosing a route, gather the information needed to understand the full picture:

  • Recent IRS notices and a current account transcript or balance information.
  • Copies of filed returns and details of any missing tax years.
  • Current income, household expenses, and available assets.
  • Existing payment agreements, liens, levies, or collection deadlines.
  • Any changes that affect your ability to pay, such as reduced income or a major expense.

Frequently asked questions

Is the IRS Fresh Start Program free?

There is no single Fresh Start enrollment fee. Some IRS options, such as an eligible short-term payment plan, have no setup fee. Other arrangements may have setup fees, application payments, interest, or penalties.

Do I have to hire a tax relief company?

No. Many taxpayers can review their options and apply directly with the IRS. Professional help may be useful when the facts are complicated or collection action is involved, but it is a separate service with fees that vary.

Can the IRS settle my tax debt for less than I owe?

Potentially. An Offer in Compromise may allow a qualifying taxpayer to settle for less than the full balance. The IRS reviews financial circumstances and does not accept every application.

Will interest stop when I enter a payment plan?

Generally, interest and applicable penalties continue until the balance is paid in full. Check the IRS rules and your account for the charges that apply to your debt.

Can low-income taxpayers avoid IRS setup or application fees?

Some low-income taxpayers may qualify for a waiver or reimbursement of certain installment agreement fees. Qualifying low-income applicants may also be exempt from the Offer in Compromise application fee and initial payment. The requirements differ by option.

Where can I check current IRS fees?

Use the IRS's official payment-plan and Offer in Compromise pages. Fee schedules and rules can change, so verify them before submitting an application.
